title: "The EU Return Regulation: from 20% enforcement to a common European system"
date: 2026-08-15
topic: Migration
tldr: "A new Return Regulation, agreed by Parliament and Council in June 2026 and approved by Parliament in plenary on 17 June, replaces the 2008 Returns Directive with a standardised EU order, detention of up to 30 months, and the option of offshore return hubs — though formal Council adoption is still pending."
where_things_stand: >
  The Commission proposed the Return Regulation in March 2025 as the enforcement complement to the Pact on Migration and Asylum, which became fully applicable across the EU on 12 June 2026. The Council reached its general approach in December 2025, and Parliament approved the start of negotiations in a plenary vote on 26 March 2026 (389 in favour, 206 against, 32 abstentions). After four trilogues that stalled over the date of application, the Council and Parliament struck a political agreement on 1 June 2026, and Parliament formally approved the text on 17 June (418 in favour, 218 against, 30 abstentions). As of mid-August 2026, the regulation still needs final adoption by the Council before publication in the Official Journal, after which it enters into force the following day, with a 12-month implementation period for most articles.
what_is_at_stake: >
  The regulation replaces the 2008 Returns Directive with a directly applicable Common European System for Returns, built around a standardised European Return Order that other member states must recognise. The agreed text allows detention of up to 30 months for people who refuse to leave — well above the 18-month ceiling under the old directive, and higher than earlier figures floated during the negotiation, a sign of how far the text moved during trilogue. It also opens the door to "return hubs" in third countries as either final destinations or transfer centres. A frontrunner group of five governments — the Netherlands, Austria, Germany, Greece and Denmark — has already pushed the offshore-hub model. Human rights organisations have flagged the expanded search and investigative powers the regulation grants national authorities, including entry into homes and shelters run by humanitarian organisations, as among its most contested provisions.
why_it_matters: >
  For member states, the core problem the regulation targets is real: the Commission estimates only around 20 to 28 percent of return orders are currently enforced, and a standardised order plus mutual recognition between states is meant to close that gap. For third-country nationals in return proceedings, existing safeguards — non-refoulement under the Refugee Convention and the EU Charter, the right to an effective remedy, and judicial review of detention — remain formally in place, but the longer detention ceiling and the return-hub model shift the practical balance toward faster enforcement over individualised review. For third countries approached as potential hosts of return hubs, the regulation creates a new category of bilateral negotiation with the EU that did not exist under the old directive. The final vote itself is also a political signal: it passed on an EPP-and-far-right coalition, with the centre-left, Greens and the Left opposed, a marker of how far the median Parliament position has shifted on enforcement since the 2025 proposal.
what_to_watch: >
  Formal Council adoption and Official Journal publication are the next formal steps before the implementation clock starts running for the provisions that are not immediately applicable. Given that the return-hub model depends on agreements with third countries willing to host them, the real test of this file will be diplomatic as much as legal: how many third countries the Commission and the frontrunner group can actually bring on board, and how courts, including potentially the Court of Justice of the EU, treat the 30-month detention ceiling and the expanded search powers once the regulation starts producing enforcement decisions.
