## Political-institutional overview

The original CSRD substantially expanded both the number of companies required to report sustainability information and the depth of that reporting, bringing it closer to the financial-reporting regime. Omnibus I has reversed much of that ambition: the applicable threshold rises from 250 employees and €50 million in turnover to more than 1,000 employees and €450 million in net turnover, a change that, according to legal-sector estimates, removes around 80% of the companies previously within scope. Non-EU parent groups are brought into scope only where an EU subsidiary or branch generates more than €200 million in net turnover, on top of a group-level threshold that itself tripled from €150 million to €450 million.

The practical effect differs sharply by wave. Wave 1 companies, already reporting since 2025 on FY2024 data, continue largely unaffected. Wave 2 companies received a two-year delay under the 2025 "Stop-the-Clock" Directive, but that delay is now largely academic since most of them no longer clear the new, higher thresholds at all. Wave 3, listed SMEs and small, non-complex credit institutions, falls outside mandatory scope entirely rather than merely being delayed. Obligations for companies that remain within scope apply from financial years beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with first reports due in 2028.

## Map of Actors

**European Commission.** DG FISMA leads this dossier within the financial-services and sustainability portfolio; EFRAG acts as the technical advisory body tasked with drafting the simplified standards. The Commission adopted the delegated act revising the ESRS on 3 July 2026, ahead of its own 18 September deadline.

**European Parliament.** The same Omnibus I package that amended the CSDDD also amended the CSRD, with Jörgen Warborn (EPP, Sweden) as JURI rapporteur for both dossiers.

**Council.** The Polish Presidency (first half of 2025) negotiated the Council's initial mandate for the Omnibus.

**Member states.** Early transposition choices are already visible: Greece transposed the Stop-the-Clock Directive via Law 5255/2025 in a way that keeps Wave 1 companies fully under CSRD without any relief, while treating Wave 2 and Wave 3 companies according to the EU-level delay.

## Advanced political analysis

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">01</span><span class="read-card-title">One package, one story, two directives</span></div>
<p>The CSRD simplification cannot be read in isolation: it was processed within the same legislative package as the CSDDD, with the same rapporteur and the same timetable, suggesting a deliberate Commission strategy to present both dossiers as a single "reduction of administrative burden" package rather than as two separate substantive debates.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">02</span><span class="read-card-title">A delay that became irrelevant</span></div>
<p>The Stop-the-Clock Directive was negotiated and adopted in April 2025 as a genuine two-year postponement for Wave 2 and 3 companies. By the time Omnibus I's scope changes landed in February 2026, most of those companies had been removed from scope altogether, so the delay several institutions fought over now applies to a much smaller group than intended when it was written.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">03</span><span class="read-card-title">National transposition is where the real variation will show up</span></div>
<p>Greece's early transposition choice, protecting Wave 1 companies from any Stop-the-Clock relief while applying the EU-level delay to Waves 2 and 3, is a first data point on how member states will use their discretion. With 27 transposition processes still ahead, this is likely to be the layer where the directive's practical effect diverges most between countries.</p>
</div>
