## Political-institutional overview

The DNA follows the same institutional logic as the PPWR: it replaces a directive-based regime, the European Electronic Communications Code, with a single, directly applicable Regulation, ending the possibility of divergent national transposition. It also consolidates the BEREC Regulation, core parts of the Open Internet Regulation, elements of the ePrivacy Directive and the Radio Spectrum Policy Programme into one instrument, and creates a new EU body, the Office for Digital Networks (ODN), which absorbs the functions of the former BEREC Office.

The centrepiece is a "single passport" authorisation system: a provider wishing to operate in more than one member state would submit a single notification to one national regulator, which forwards it through the ODN to the other member states concerned. A parallel EU-level authorisation is introduced for satellite spectrum, and the text mandates a copper-switch-off in areas meeting defined conditions. The proposal reached publication only after the Commission reworked it following a negative Regulatory Scrutiny Board opinion on the draft impact assessment in late October 2025, a delay that itself signals the political sensitivity of ceding national regulatory competence in this area.

A less-noticed feature ties the DNA to the Cybersecurity Act 2.0, published by the Commission just one day earlier: compliance with CSA supply-chain security measures becomes a condition for general authorisations and spectrum rights under the DNA, linking the two files' substance and, likely, their legislative timelines.

## Map of Actors

**European Commission.** DG CNECT leads the dossier, framing it as necessary to fund next-generation 5G/6G and fibre infrastructure and to help EU operators compete at scale with US and Asian players.

**European Parliament.** ITRE is the lead committee, with IMCO and LIBE associated. Michał Kobosko (Renew, Poland) holds the rapporteurship, a notable outcome given the EPP did not secure it, leaving Pilar del Castillo Vera (EPP, Spain) in a shadow role. S&D shadows Elena Sancho Murillo and Matthias Ecke, with Ecke seen as the more influential of the two, and ECR's Alessandro Torselli and Susanna Solier are weighing which of them takes this file versus the parallel Cybersecurity Act.

**Council.** The six-state intervention of December 2025 (Austria, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Slovenia) set the tone for Council scrutiny. Working-party sessions through mid-2026 show a second recurring concern beyond spectrum control: several delegations, while welcoming the simplification ambition, have questioned the proposal's reliance on implementing acts and BEREC guidelines, warning this could weaken legal predictability.

**External stakeholders.** BEREC's early assessment, published 30 March 2026, is more critical than a routine technical input: it welcomes the DNA's ambition but states plainly that several key provisions add unnecessary complexity without clear added value, and does not consider the text achieves its own stated simplification goals in those areas. On the industry side, the Computer & Communications Industry Association has criticised the DNA as a "backdoor" route to reviving network-fee proposals, the long-running dispute over whether large content and cloud providers should help fund telecom infrastructure costs, a fight the DNA's authorisation and resilience provisions touch indirectly.

## Advanced political analysis

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">01</span><span class="read-card-title">The pushback came before the text existed</span></div>
<p>Six member states raised objections in December 2025, a month before the Commission even published the proposal. That kind of pre-publication pressure is unusual and suggests the Commission will need to make real concessions on spectrum governance to secure a Council mandate, rather than treating national resistance as a negotiating formality to be managed after the fact.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">02</span><span class="read-card-title">BEREC's criticism cuts against the Commission's own framing</span></div>
<p>The Commission presents the DNA as simplification. BEREC, the body of national regulators asked to help implement it, says several provisions do the opposite. When the technical regulator most familiar with the current framework disputes the simplification claim directly, that carries more weight in Council and ITRE negotiations than a stakeholder objecting on principle.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">03</span><span class="read-card-title">A quiet second front: network fees via the back door</span></div>
<p>The CCIA's "backdoor" framing points to a genuine substantive link: DNA provisions on resilience and authorisation touch the years-old dispute over whether large content and cloud providers should contribute to network costs, a fight the EU tried and failed to settle definitively in 2023. If that dispute re-attaches itself to the DNA, it brings a much larger and better-resourced set of lobbying actors into a file currently framed mostly around spectrum and authorisation.</p>
</div>

<div class="dsection dsection-transposition" markdown="1">

## Transposition by country

Not applicable in the traditional sense: as a Regulation, the DNA does not require national transposition. What is relevant to track is how national regulatory authorities reorganise as coordination functions shift toward the ODN, and how member states respond to the loss of "gold-plating" discretion on authorisation conditions.
