## Political-institutional overview

What the Commission tabled on 19 November 2025 as a single "digital package" split, in practice, into two files that have moved at very different speeds. The AI Omnibus amends the AI Act and was fast-tracked ahead of the 2 August 2026 deadline for high-risk-system obligations, reaching adoption in a little over eight months. The Data Omnibus, touching the GDPR, the ePrivacy Directive, NIS2, DORA and the Platform-to-Business Regulation, and consolidating the Data Governance Act, the Open Data Directive and the Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation into a restructured Data Act, has no equivalent legal cliff-edge forcing a timetable. It has now stalled outright: after the Cypriot Presidency dropped the most contested cookie-consent provisions to try to secure a mandate, Germany blocked it anyway on 26 June, handing the file to the incoming Irish Presidency unresolved.

The package is presented by the Commission as the first step in a "stress test" of the EU digital rulebook running through the 2024–2029 mandate, with further sectoral reviews (Digital Markets Act, Digital Decade, Chips Act, Copyright Directive) already pencilled in for 2026–2029.

## Map of Actors

**European Commission.** DG CNECT leads on the AI and connectivity elements; DG JUST is closely involved on the GDPR amendments. Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen has been the public face of the package, framing it explicitly as removing "regulatory clutter" rather than lowering protections.

**European Parliament, AI track.** The AI Omnibus was jointly handled by IMCO and LIBE, with Arba Kokalari (EPP, Sweden) and Michael McNamara (Renew, Ireland) as rapporteurs; the March 2026 committee vote (101-9-8) was comfortable, but plenary support narrowed markedly by June (423-57, with abstentions nearly quadrupling versus the March mandate vote), suggesting simplification became more politically contested as the AI Omnibus advanced.

**European Parliament, Data track.** The Data Omnibus was referred jointly to ITRE and LIBE, with Aura Salla (EPP, Finland) as rapporteur for ITRE and Marina Kaljurand (S&D, Estonia) for LIBE. Kaljurand has already publicly challenged the provision letting AI systems process sensitive data for bias detection and correction, arguing it would end the "technological neutrality" of the GDPR.

**Council.** The Council adopted its AI Omnibus mandate on 13 March 2026 and gave final approval on 29 June 2026. On the Data Omnibus, the Cypriot Presidency circulated successive compromise texts through May and June, ultimately dropping the cookie-consent and AI-legitimate-interest provisions (Articles 88a-c) on 10 June to try to save the mandate. Germany blocked it regardless on 26 June. As early as March 2026, France, Poland and Romania had already submitted written objections to folding cookie-consent rules into the GDPR at all.

**Institutional watchdogs and civil society.** The EDPB and EDPS issued joint opinions on both files, 20 January 2026 on the AI Omnibus and 11 February 2026 on the GDPR elements of the Data Omnibus, flagging risks to the architecture of accountability built up under existing law. The privacy NGO noyb published a running legal analysis of the Data Omnibus (three versions between November 2025 and February 2026) arguing the changes weaken data-subject protections. From the opposite direction, the industry-aligned think tank ICLE argued the reform actually hands more interpretive power to regulators rather than genuinely simplifying anything, a critique of the drafting rather than of deregulation as such.

## Advanced political analysis

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">01</span><span class="read-card-title">Deadlines discipline negotiations, their absence lets them drift</span></div>
<p>The AI Omnibus had a hard external deadline, the AI Act's own 2 August 2026 high-risk application date, that forced all three institutions into an eight-month sprint. The Data Omnibus has no comparable trigger, and has been left to move at the pace of ordinary Council consensus-building, which turned out to mean no pace at all once Germany objected.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">02</span><span class="read-card-title">Dropping the hardest provisions was not enough</span></div>
<p>The Cypriot Presidency's decision to delete the cookie-consent and AI-legitimate-interest articles on 10 June was a direct attempt to buy Germany's support. It failed anyway on 26 June, which suggests the German-led opposition runs deeper than those two provisions, and that a revived mandate under Ireland will need more than further deletions to pass.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">03</span><span class="read-card-title">Critics on both flanks, for different reasons</span></div>
<p>Industry and privacy advocates rarely agree, and here they both find fault, just not with the same clause. noyb reads the proposed changes as a rollback of GDPR safeguards; ICLE reads the same drafting as failing to actually reduce regulators' discretion. Both readings surviving in the public record simultaneously is itself a sign the current text has not resolved the substantive trade-off it claims to.</p>
</div>

## Who is affected

<div class="impact-grid">

  <div class="impact-card exposure-direct">
    <div class="impact-card-head">
      <span class="impact-card-name">AI providers &amp; deployers</span>
      <span class="impact-tag">Settled</span>
    </div>
    <p class="impact-card-text">The 16-month deferral for standalone high-risk (Annex III) systems to December 2027, and the 12-month deferral to August 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products, gives real breathing room. Article 50 transparency obligations were not delayed and applied from 2 August 2026, alongside a watermarking carve-out running to December 2026.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="impact-card exposure-direct">
    <div class="impact-card-head">
      <span class="impact-card-name">Data controllers &amp; processors</span>
      <span class="impact-tag">Unresolved</span>
    </div>
    <p class="impact-card-text">The GDPR and ePrivacy elements remain unresolved after the 26 June Council block, so no compliance planning can yet be based on a final text. The proposed one-click, six-month cookie consent and browser-level saved preferences remain on the table but unagreed.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="impact-card exposure-operational">
    <div class="impact-card-head">
      <span class="impact-card-name">SMEs</span>
      <span class="impact-tag">Operational</span>
    </div>
    <p class="impact-card-text">Both files are framed around reducing administrative burden disproportionately borne by smaller companies, though the Data Omnibus is where the bulk of that burden actually sits, and it is the half of the package that is stuck.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="impact-card exposure-oversight">
    <div class="impact-card-head">
      <span class="impact-card-name">Data protection authorities</span>
      <span class="impact-tag">Oversight</span>
    </div>
    <p class="impact-card-text">The EDPB and EDPS have already flagged risks to the existing architecture of accountability. A single reporting point for cybersecurity and data incidents, if agreed, would also reshape how authorities receive breach notifications across NIS2 and DORA at once.</p>
  </div>
