## Political-institutional overview

ESRS are the detailed technical development of what the CSRD requires: the directive sets the legal framework, ESRS specify exactly what data must be reported. Their revision has run in parallel to the political negotiation of Omnibus I, delegated technically to EFRAG (European Financial Reporting Advisory Group) under a Commission mandate.

On 3 July 2026 the Commission adopted both the revised ESRS and a new voluntary sustainability standard for smaller companies (VSME), intended for companies that fall outside the CSRD's mandatory scope after Omnibus I but still want to report in a harmonised way, for example because they sit in the supply chain of a company that is required to report. The revision removes roughly 70% of the previous mandatory datapoints and is expected to cut reporting costs by more than 30% per entity, according to the Commission's own estimate.

</div>

<div class="dsection dsection-actors" markdown="1">

## Map of Actors

**EFRAG.** The Commission's technical advisory body, responsible for drafting the detailed standards proposal. It is not a EU institution in the strict sense, but a private, not-for-profit body operating under delegated mandate. EFRAG delivered its final technical advice in December 2025, after publishing exposure drafts in July 2025.

**European Commission.** DG FISMA formalises EFRAG's technical proposal as a delegated act, and closely followed EFRAG's advice in its own May 2026 draft, with some additional simplifications of its own.

**Parliament and Council.** Both now hold the revised ESRS and the VSME for a scrutiny period of up to four months, running from the 3 July 2026 adoption. Their role, as with other delegated acts in this package, is scrutiny and potential objection, not line-by-line negotiation.

**External stakeholders.** KPMG's Jan A. Müller welcomed the revision as an important step but stressed the need for market stability going forward, arguing the framework should now be allowed to mature "without ongoing uncertainty caused by frequent changes to scope and requirements," a view echoed informally by several other advisory firms tracking the file.

## Advanced political analysis

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">01</span><span class="read-card-title">A technical debate, not a political one</span></div>
<p>Unlike the CSRD and the CSDDD, the debate over ESRS has been predominantly technical rather than political: the discussion is about the practical usability of indicators, not their existence. Even so, the final outcome is shaped by the political decisions taken in the CSRD, which companies are in scope, at what depth, so the two files need to be read together.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">02</span><span class="read-card-title">The numbers do the talking</span></div>
<p>A roughly 70% cut in total datapoints and a 30%-plus reduction in reporting costs are unusually concrete simplification figures compared with the more qualitative claims made on other Omnibus files. That level of quantification, and the fact the Commission's draft tracked EFRAG's advice closely, is itself evidence of a high degree of consensus behind the final adopted text.</p>
</div>

<div class="read-card">
<div class="read-card-head"><span class="read-card-num">03</span><span class="read-card-title">A quiet legal risk sits behind the scrutiny period</span></div>
<p>Beyond the Parliament and Council scrutiny window, companies in CSRD scope will have a two-month-and-ten-day window after Official Journal publication to challenge the delegated regulation before the EU's General Court. It is a procedural detail rather than a live dispute today, but it is the one point in an otherwise low-friction file where a legal challenge could still emerge.</p>
</div>
