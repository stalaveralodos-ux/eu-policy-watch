## Political-institutional overview

What the Commission tabled on 19 November 2025 as a single "digital package" split, in practice, into two files that have moved at very different speeds. The AI Omnibus amends the AI Act and was fast-tracked ahead of the 2 August 2026 deadline for high-risk-system obligations, reaching adoption in a little over eight months. The Data Omnibus — touching the GDPR, the ePrivacy Directive, NIS2 and DORA, and consolidating the Data Governance Act, the Open Data Directive and the Free Flow of Non-Personal Data Regulation into a restructured Data Act — has no equivalent legal cliff-edge forcing a timetable, and remains in Council working-group negotiation as of mid-August 2026. The package is presented by the Commission as the first step in a "stress test" of the EU digital rulebook running through the 2024–2029 mandate, with further sectoral reviews (Digital Markets Act, Digital Decade, Chips Act, Copyright Directive) already pencilled in for 2026–2029.

## Map of Actors

**European Commission.** DG CNECT leads on the AI and connectivity elements; DG JUST is closely involved on the GDPR amendments. Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen has been the public face of the package, framing it explicitly as removing "regulatory clutter" rather than lowering protections.

**European Parliament.** The AI Omnibus was jointly handled by IMCO and LIBE, with Arba Kokalari (EPP, Sweden) and Michael McNamara (Renew, Ireland) as rapporteurs; the March 2026 committee vote (101-9-8) was comfortable, but plenary support narrowed markedly by June (423-57, with abstentions nearly quadrupling versus the March mandate vote), suggesting simplification became more politically contested as the AI Omnibus advanced. **Council.** The Council adopted its AI Omnibus mandate on 13 March 2026 and gave final approval on 29 June 2026. On the Data Omnibus, the Cypriot Presidency has circulated successive compromise texts (most recently 21 May 2026), with the cookie-consent and GDPR provisions the most contested.

**Institutional watchdogs.** The EDPB and EDPS issued joint opinions on both files — 20 January 2026 on the AI Omnibus and 11 February 2026 on the GDPR elements of the Data Omnibus — flagging risks to the architecture of accountability built up under existing law.

## Advanced political analysis

The divergence between the two tracks is itself the most useful analytical fact about this dossier: legislative deadlines shape negotiating dynamics as much as substance does. The AI Omnibus had a hard external deadline (the AI Act's own 2 August 2026 high-risk application date) that disciplined all three institutions into an eight-month sprint; the Data Omnibus has no comparable trigger, and has been left to move at the pace of ordinary Council consensus-building — considerably slower. Both political groups pushing the Omnibus and civil-society critics can be correct about the same clause simultaneously: industry read the fast AI-track outcome as overdue relief from obligations landing faster than compliance capacity allowed, while critics read the same deferral as a rollback of safeguards negotiated as such rather than as red tape.

## Impact by sector

**AI providers and deployers.** The 16-month deferral for standalone high-risk (Annex III) systems to December 2027, and the 12-month deferral to August 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products, gives real breathing room — but Article 50 transparency obligations were not delayed and applied from 2 August 2026, with only a watermarking carve-out (to December 2026) for systems already on the market. **Data controllers and processors.** The GDPR and ePrivacy elements remain unresolved, so no compliance planning can yet be based on a final text. **SMEs.** Both files are framed around reducing administrative burden disproportionately borne by smaller companies, though the Data Omnibus is where the bulk of that burden actually sits.
